Repairability is a spec you can price

The price tag sits on the front of the box. Lifespan hides on the inside, and repairability is the slice of it a buyer can actually act on. The DurablePicks espresso machine piece spends twenty years on boiler metallurgy and E61 serviceability because the machines that last are the ones that open. The mug ranking runs the same question from the other end, sorted by how things die. Failure mode says what broke. Serviceability says whether that ends the product.

The sealed box is a subscription

Run one purchase as arithmetic. A sealed unit at $90 that dies around year three needs five purchases across fifteen years, $450 all in. A serviceable unit at $150 with a single $22 pump swap at year eight spends $172 over the same span. The cheaper register price costs more than double, and none of that showed at checkout. This is the same math the cost-per-decade reviews run on socks and duffels, applied one purchase at a time.

Tells that read from the product page

Two signals show up before any purchase. A published parts diagram with priced components means the maker expects the unit to be opened again. Fasteners read the same way: standard screws assume a repair, a glued seam assumes a replacement. Both of these cost the maker money up front, which is why they track with lifespan better than any marketing line about durability.

The habit is small. Before checkout, look up the parts diagram. If it exists and the parts carry prices, the lifespan claims in the review mean something. If the unit is a sealed brick, price it as a subscription with a three-year term and compare from there.
